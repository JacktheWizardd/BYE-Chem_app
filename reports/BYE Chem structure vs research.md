# BYE Chem's order holds; practice lags

Research gives no reason to reorder BYE Chem's chapters, and the page layout is strongest at signposting and weakest at practice. **No study tests most of the order choices directly.** The few studies that compare whole course orders find small, inconsistent effects, all mixed up with changes of teacher, exam and textbook. So the fair test is whether textbooks and course frameworks back each choice, and whether the beginner research agrees with the reasoning. On that test, **six of the nine distinctive order choices match at least one published book or framework.** Three have no verified precedent. Bonds and shapes before quantum is the first, though the intro course's content list supports it. The split of the two bonding chapters, which follows from that choice, is the second. Solutions before Solids is the third. Research contradicts none of the three, and bonds-first is the riskiest. The page layout is well grounded: the readiness check, segmented lessons, guided Lab challenges, three views and the planned fading all match good evidence. But it leaves out the four habits with the best evidence of all: **getting learners to recall answers, spacing review over time, mixing problem types, and giving feedback on answers.** Since the worked problems are still on hold, this is the best moment to add them. The verdicts below are **keep** (leave as is), **change** (fix before building) or **consider** (worth testing or improving, not urgent). Evidence strength is rated **Strong** (many studies agree), **Moderate** (several studies, with limits), **Weak** (one or two small studies, theory or opinion) or **None found**. Anything marked UNVERIFIED came from search summaries or memory and needs checking before it is quoted.

## Topic order barely moves results, so books and frameworks set the bar

What BYE Chem is, first. Its chapter map runs Chapter 0 (Maths for Chemistry), then matter, atoms, compounds and a separate mole chapter (4). Reactions (5), Reactions in Water with a first look at pH (6.7), Gases (7) and Energy in Reactions (8) follow. Bonds and Shapes (9) come before Light and the Electron (10), The Periodic Table Explained (11) and Why Bonds Form (12). Then Liquids and Solids (13), Solutions (14), Solids and Materials (15) and Carbon Compounds (16), which close the 1A half. The 1B half runs kinetics, equilibrium, acids and bases, buffers, free energy, electrochemistry, nuclear and the elements ([chapter map](../docs/chapter-map.md)). The app mock-up has a Home tab with three ways in ("Find your gaps", "Learn from zero", "I'm taking Chem 1A"). Each chapter page has a one-line promise, "What this lets you do" with a particle picture, "Ready? You need", the numbered lessons, then a Lab link, "The chapter at a glance" and "Where it shows up". Each section page has Learn, Simple, Medium and Hard tabs, with "one quantity, three ways" (See it, Particles, Symbols) on the Learn tab ([design mock-up](../design/chem-chapters.html)). Fully worked → partly worked → solo fading is planned, and worked problems are on hold ([research summary](../docs/research/README.md)).

The order research is thin. The best-known comparisons test "atoms first" (quantum and atomic structure at the start) against the traditional "reactions first" order. In a five-year study of **more than 8,000 students**, fewer atoms-first students passed at first ([Esterling & Bartels 2013](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1028492); the study and its JCE issue are confirmed, but its exact conclusions come from summaries and are UNVERIFIED). A three-year study of about 600 students found atoms-first results "generally favorable" only for atomic and molecular structure topics ([Chitiyo et al. 2018](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1195639)). An unreviewed dissertation covering **more than 9,500 students** found higher final-exam scores and grades for the *traditional* order ([Hillesheim 2016](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/td/1120)). All of these are single-campus studies with no random assignment. A 25-year review of what lowers fail-and-withdraw rates credits active learning, a core-concepts curriculum and low-stakes testing, not topic order (details UNVERIFIED, summary only: [J. Chem. Educ. record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2800248546)). The CLUE curriculum reports better skill with Lewis structures and earlier links between structure and properties ([Underwood et al. 2016](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlelanding/2016/rp/c5rp00217f)), and Michigan State reports a pass rate about 20% higher after switching to it ([MSU Today](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2015/msu-chemistry-courses-improve-learning-save-students-money)). But CLUE changed the teaching methods and tests along with the order, so its gains can't be credited to order alone ([Cooper & Klymkowsky 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3671656)).

The frameworks don't require an order either. The College Board calls its nine AP units "an optional sequence" ([AP Chemistry CED](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf)). The ACS content map (ACCM) groups content under ten "big ideas" for exam alignment and is not a teaching order ([ACS Exams ACCM project](https://uwm.edu/acs-exams/instructors/research-development/anchoring-concepts-content-map-accm-project/)). The C-ID descriptors list required content, not a sequence. CHEM 110 adds that topics "may be revisited in semester two" ([C-ID CHEM 110](https://data-c-idsystem.org/descriptors/final/show/297), as recorded in the project's [course research](../docs/research/A-course.md)). The ten ACCM concept names were not retrieved and are UNVERIFIED. **So any order is legitimate if it covers the content.** The practical risks are a student getting lost when the class follows a different order, and an early simplified model never being corrected later.

## Maths first, the mole alone and bonds first are the bold choices

| Choice | What books and frameworks do | Research | Strength | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chapter 0 maths first | No book has a Chapter 0. Maths sits in ch 1–2 or an appendix | Maths skill predicts grades. A review raises maths scores, not course grades | Strong (prediction), Weak (review helps grades) | **Keep**, emphasise arithmetic fluency |
| The mole as its own chapter | Rare in general chemistry books. Some intro books and AP give it its own place | The mole is a documented bottleneck, worst for weaker maths students | Moderate (difficulty), None (chapter format) | **Keep** |
| Bonds and shapes before quantum | No verified general or intro book. AP puts electron configuration first | No direct test. Atoms-first studies are mixed, and CLUE supports early Lewis-to-property work | Weak, indirect | **Keep**, with a planned bridge |
| VSEPR (9) and Why Bonds Form (12) split by 10–11 | Every verified book keeps the two bonding chapters together | Critics say VSEPR and hybridization already feel unrelated | Weak | **Change**: add an explicit bridge into Ch 12 |

**Chapter 0, maths first.** No general or intro textbook in the sample has a stand-alone maths chapter. Tro 6e puts maths in Appendix I, "Common Mathematical Operations in Chemistry" ([Tro 6e contents](../docs/research/M-tro-6e-contents.md)). Tro's *Structure and Properties* opens with an "Essentials: Units, Measurement, and Problem Solving" unit ([Pearson](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/chemistry-structure-and-properties/P200000009744/9780138095505)). OpenStax ends Chapter 1 with "Mathematical Treatment of Measurement Results" ([OpenStax 2e ch 1](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/1-introduction)). The research case for putting maths first is strong on one point: maths skill predicts success. A 15-minute, calculator-free arithmetic test given to **1,073 students at six institutions** tracked course grades ([Williamson et al. 2020](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2020/rp/c9rp00077a)). Formal reasoning and SAT scores each flag at-risk students in samples of **more than 3,000** ([Lewis & Lewis 2007](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2007/rp/b6rp90018f)). The case is weak on whether a review then *raises grades*. A week-2 maths review raised arithmetic scores more than a chemistry review did, but final course averages did not differ ([Alivio et al. 2020](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........7154474eb2eca74dde0bdcf31e409899)). Nobody has directly compared up-front and point-of-use maths review. BYE Chem already uses the mix the evidence fits best: four short sections first (0.1–0.4), with logarithms, quadratics and graphs held until a "Ready?" check sends the learner there. **Keep it**, and centre 0.1–0.4 on the documented predictor: fast, calculator-free arithmetic (fractions, negatives, powers of ten), not harder algebra. One fix is needed in the map. Section 6.7 (pH, a First Look) is tagged Intro and lists 0.5 Logarithms as a prerequisite, but 0.5 is tagged only 1A, 1B and AP ([chapter map](../docs/chapter-map.md)). An Intro student would be sent to a section their course tag hides. Either tag 0.5 for Intro or make 6.7 work without logs.

**The mole as its own chapter.** Among general chemistry books, none of the verified tables of contents names the mole as a chapter. Brown, Zumdahl and Silberberg fold it into a single stoichiometry chapter. OpenStax 2e comes closest with "Composition of Substances and Solutions" (ch 3), separate from reaction stoichiometry (ch 4) ([OpenStax 2e](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openstax/osbooks-chemistry-bundle/main/collections/chemistry-2e.collection.xml)). That the mole sits inside that chapter is UNVERIFIED at section level. Intro books are friendlier to the idea. Corwin has a chapter titled "The Mole Concept" ([Corwin 8e](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/introductory-chemistry-concepts-and-critical-thinking/P200000006817/9780134553146)). Tro's *Introductory Chemistry* puts "Chemical Composition" (ch 6) *before* "Chemical Reactions" (ch 7) ([Tro Intro 7e](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/introductory-chemistry/P200000009745/9780138198411)). That matches BYE Chem's mole-then-reactions order, though the mole's place inside that chapter comes from the title only (UNVERIFIED). This corrects one research note, which said BYE's mole-before-reactions order was unlike all four intro books. AP goes further and makes "Moles and Molar Mass" the course's very first topic, three units before stoichiometry ([AP CED](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf)). The research backs extra time on the mole without testing chapter formats. The mole is a long-documented difficulty, partly because teaching blurs amount, mass and number of particles ([Furió et al. 2000](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/e.joling/vakdidactiek/documenten/furio.pdf)). Students in the bottom quarter on maths aptitude show their biggest gaps on mole and stoichiometry items ([Ralph & Lewis 2018](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/Content/ArticleLanding/RP/2018/C8RP00115D)). An earlier project summary of the same paper notes that at-risk students who did well on these topics went on to perform like their peers ([C-learning notes](../docs/research/C-learning.md)). **Keep.** The cost is small: the "I'm taking Chem 1A" path already maps Tro 2.9 and 3.8–3.10 onto Chapter 4.

**Bonds and shapes before quantum.** This is BYE Chem's biggest departure. Every verified general chemistry book teaches electron structure before Lewis structures and VSEPR. That includes Tro 6e (ch 8 quantum, ch 10–11 bonding) ([Tro 6e contents](../docs/research/M-tro-6e-contents.md)), Brown ([Pearson](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/chemistry-the-central-science/P200000006933)), Zumdahl ([WebAssign](https://www.webassign.net/features/textbooks/zumchem10/details.html)), Silberberg ([McGraw Hill](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/chemistry-the-molecular-nature-of-matter-and-change-silberberg.html)) and both OpenStax editions. The four intro books also place an electrons chapter before bonding, judging by titles only. Whether those chapters go as deep as orbitals is UNVERIFIED. AP puts electron configuration (1.5) before Lewis diagrams (2.5) and VSEPR (2.7). The argument *for* BYE Chem's order comes from the courses. The intro course, C-ID CHEM 101, requires "chemical bonds" and "molecular structure" but not quantum theory, while CHEM 110 adds quantum theory ([C-ID CHEM 101](https://data-c-idsystem.org/descriptors/final/show/432), via [course research](../docs/research/A-course.md)). So an intro learner needs shapes without orbitals, and group numbers give valence electrons. No study compares "Lewis/VSEPR first" with "quantum first" directly. The nearest evidence is mixed: the atoms-first studies above are a caution against putting abstract quantum content up front for weaker students. CLUE treats Lewis structures as models that predict properties and teaches that link early ([Cooper & Klymkowsky 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3671656)). A reported CLUE gain in building Lewis structures (effect size r = 0.6) is UNVERIFIED. **Keep**, as a deliberate choice rather than a proven one. Two conditions apply. Chapter 9 must not teach anything Chapter 10 later has to undo, such as "electrons orbit in rings". And Chapter 9 should reach one property quickly (polarity → boiling point), as CLUE does, instead of waiting until Chapter 13.

**Splitting the two bonding chapters.** Every verified book keeps its bonding chapters back to back: Tro 6e 10/11, Brown 8/9, Zumdahl 8/9, OpenStax 7/8 and Tro S&P 5/6 ([OpenStax 2e](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openstax/osbooks-chemistry-bundle/main/collections/chemistry-2e.collection.xml)). BYE puts quantum and periodic trends between VSEPR (9) and lattice energy, valence bond theory, hybridization and molecular orbitals (12). This follows from the bonds-first choice and has no precedent. The one relevant critique says textbooks already present VSEPR and hybridization as separate topics that students fail to connect ([Pinhas et al. 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ibn/ijcjnl/v16y2024i1p57.html)). A three-chapter gap makes that worse unless it is bridged on purpose. The prerequisites already point the right way: 12.4 requires 9.6 and 10.5 ([chapter map](../docs/chapter-map.md)). **Change** one thing: open Chapter 12 with a short recall of Chapter 9 ("you drew water as bent, now see why"). That bridge also works as a spaced review. One side effect matters for the Chem 1A path: Tro chapter 10 maps to 9.1–9.5 plus 12.1–12.3. A student following the class book jumps between two BYE chapters, and the Tro-order Home list should present that as one unit.

## The later chapters stay close to the class book

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Gases before thermochemistry | Tro 6e, Zumdahl, Silberberg, OpenStax AF, AP do the same. Brown, OpenStax 2e, Tro S&P do the reverse | None found | None found | **Keep** |
| Solutions (14) before Solids (15) | No verified precedent. Tro 6e, Brown and Tro S&P do Solids first | None found | None found | **Keep** (no shared prerequisites) |
| Organic closes the 1A half (16) | Silberberg ch 15 before kinetics. Others put organic near the end. AP has none | None found | None found | **Keep** (course fit) |
| Equilibrium before acids and bases | Every general chemistry book and AP. Intro books do the reverse | No direct study. The logic of prerequisites supports it | Weak | **Keep** |

**pH, a first look.** Tro 6e teaches pH at 17.5 ([Tro 6e contents](../docs/research/M-tro-6e-contents.md)). AP introduces acid–base reactions (4.8) and titration (4.6) in Unit 4 but leaves pH to Unit 8 ([AP CED](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-course-and-exam-description.pdf)). Every intro book in the sample has an "Acids and Bases" chapter, for example Tro Intro ch 14 and Ball ch 12 ([Ball, LibreTexts](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_Chemistry/Beginning_Chemistry_(Ball))), and C-ID 101 requires "acids, bases and salts". An intro learner who stops before Chapter 19 still needs pH, which justifies 6.7. Where general chemistry books first mention pH was not checked (UNVERIFIED). The research support is theory, not trials. Learning-progression work argues for revisiting ideas at rising depth ([Sevian & Talanquer 2014](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2014/rp/c3rp00111c)), but no controlled study of "first look, full treatment later" turned up. The known risk is a simple early model that is never revisited. The map already handles this, since 19.3 lists 6.7 as a prerequisite. **Keep**, and fix the 0.5 Logarithms tag described above.

**Gases before thermochemistry.** Here BYE Chem *follows* the class book: Tro 6e has Gases at ch 6 and Thermochemistry at ch 7 ([Tro 6e contents](../docs/research/M-tro-6e-contents.md)). Zumdahl, Silberberg and OpenStax Atoms First do the same, and AP puts gases in Unit 3 and thermochemistry in Unit 6. Brown, OpenStax 2e and Tro S&P reverse it ([Brown](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/chemistry-the-central-science/P200000006933); [OpenStax AF](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openstax/osbooks-chemistry-bundle/main/collections/chemistry-atoms-first-2e.collection.xml)). One research note said Tro 6e reverses this order, but the project's own Tro 6e contents list shows otherwise. No sequencing research exists on either order. **Keep.** CLUE's habit of threading energy through every chapter ([Cooper & Klymkowsky 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3671656)) points to one cheap addition: 1.4 "Energy, a First Look" is a good start, and Chapter 7's particle picture can say plainly that hotter particles carry more energy.

**Solutions before Solids.** No verified book does this. Tro 6e, Brown and Tro S&P run liquids and forces → solids → solutions. Silberberg and OpenStax 2e have one combined liquids-and-solids chapter, then solutions ([Silberberg](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/chemistry-the-molecular-nature-of-matter-and-change-silberberg.html)). AP puts solids (3.2) before solutions (3.7) within one unit. No research exists either way. The swap is safe by BYE's own prerequisites: nothing in Chapter 14 needs Chapter 15, and nothing in 15 needs 14 ([chapter map](../docs/chapter-map.md)). **Keep.** Since the map calls Solids "the 1A chapter needed least", consider marking it optional on the from-zero path.

**Organic at the end of the 1A half.** Silberberg is the only verified analogue: organic at ch 15, just before kinetics ([Silberberg](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/chemistry-the-molecular-nature-of-matter-and-change-silberberg.html)). Tro 6e and the rest put organic near the end, and AP leaves it out. PCC's CHEM 001A lists an "introduction to organic" ([PCC catalog](https://curriculum.pasadena.edu/course-descriptions/chem/chem.pdf)), so placing it where 1A ends fits the course. No research was found on where organic should go. **Keep.**

**Equilibrium before acids and bases.** Every general chemistry book does this (Tro 6e 16→17, Brown 15→16, Zumdahl 13→14, Silberberg 17→18, OpenStax 13→14), and so does AP (Unit 7 → Unit 8). Intro books reverse it (Tro Intro 14→15, Ball 12→13). That difference is expected: intro books teach acids without K. BYE's own prerequisites show why equilibrium must come first at this level: 19.2 needs 18.2 (K) and 19.4 needs 18.6 (ICE tables). The intro-level acid needs are covered by 6.6–6.7. **Keep.** One small point for the from-zero path: equilibrium is tagged "1A (first look)", yet it sits after Kinetics (17), which is 1B only. Sections 18.1–18.2 need only 5.1, so let 1A learners reach them without passing through Chapter 17.

## The page layout gets the signposts right and leaves out practice

| Element | What books do | Research | Strength | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Home, three ways in | Books have one order | Mastery learning ≈0.5 SD. Placement evidence is mostly from vendors | Moderate (mastery), Weak (placement) | **Keep** the doors, **change** the gap check |
| One-line promise | Openers and learning objectives | Advance organizers: small, inconsistent effects | Weak | **Keep**, short |
| "What this lets you do" + particle picture | Opening stories | Particle pictures close a well-replicated gap. Relevance works best when learners write it themselves | Strong (gap), Moderate (relevance) | **Keep**, **consider** a "write one line" prompt |
| "Ready? You need" | Rare. Tro has an appendix | Mastery logic. Pre-questions help only the content they ask about | Moderate | **Keep**, **change** to answer-first |
| Numbered lessons | Numbered sections | Segmenting, median d ≈ 0.67–0.70 | Moderate–Strong (lab studies) | **Keep** |
| Lab link with "Try this" | Rare | Guided discovery beats free play (d = 0.30) | Moderate | **Keep**, lead with the challenge |
| Chapter at a glance | End-of-chapter summary | Benefit of provided summaries not found | None found | **Consider**: turn it into a recall check |
| Where it shows up | Everyday-chemistry boxes | Told relevance is the weaker form | Weak | **Keep** |
| Learn · Simple · Medium · Hard with planned fading | Worked example → practice problem | Fading supported. Worked examples save time more than they add learning | Strong (general), Moderate (chemistry) | **Keep**, **change**: fade on performance, add feedback |
| Three views: See it · Particles · Symbols | Figures with captions | Johnstone framework. Benefits depend on linking the views | Strong (framework), Moderate (design) | **Keep**, **change**: link the views |

**Home and "Find your gaps".** Diagnosing, filling gaps, then moving on is mastery learning. One synthesis of **108 controlled evaluations** found positive exam effects. The often-quoted mean of about 0.5 SD comes from secondary summaries, and critics argue it is inflated when researchers write their own tests ([Slavin 1987](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf)). For chemistry placement, a vendor-reported Indiana University study found ALEKS Prep students as likely to succeed as students who took a semester-long prep course ([McGraw-Hill release](https://mheducation.mediaroom.com/2019-12-17-New-Research-Shows-McGraw-Hill-ALEKS-Prepares-Students-for-General-Chemistry-Course-Just-as-Effectively-as-a-Full-Semester-Prep-Intro-Course)). An independent study of a voluntary summer module found only a link with higher scores, not proof it caused them ([CERP 2021](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2021/rp/d0rp00283f)). **Keep the three doors.** A Tro-order view for Chem 1A students directly addresses the main risk of a non-standard order. **Change "Find your gaps"**: 10 questions across 26 chapters is less than one question per two chapters, a coarse filter compared with ALEKS's adaptive check, which keeps re-checking learners as they go. Report gaps by skill, not by chapter, and run a short re-check later, so a learner wrongly told to skip a chapter gets caught.

**Promise, "What this lets you do" and the particle picture.** A one-line promise works as an "advance organizer", but the evidence for written organizers is weak. A review of 135 studies found effects that varied widely ([Luiten et al. 1980](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)), so keep it to one line. The particle picture rests on firmer ground. Students who can run the numbers often fail the matching particle-picture question ([Nurrenbern & Pickering 1987](https://doi.org/10.1021/ed064p508)), and novices read the surface look of pictures unless told what to notice (Kozma & Russell 1997, in the project's [C-learning notes](../docs/research/C-learning.md)). The mole page's "The trick:" caption does exactly that. On relevance, the effect comes from learners *writing* how a topic connects to their own lives. In a randomized trial with 262 students, grades rose by about two-thirds of a letter grade for students who expected to do poorly ([Hulleman & Harackiewicz 2009](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/hulleman_harackiewicz_2009_-_promoting_interest_performance_in_high_school_science_classes.pdf)). The gain was about 0.5 grade points for first-generation, underrepresented students in intro biology (secondary summary, check the original: [Harackiewicz et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5008900)). "What this lets you do" and "Where it shows up" are the weaker, *told* form. **Keep both**, and **consider** a one-line prompt at chapter end: "Where could you use this? Write one line." The claim that told relevance can backfire for anxious students is UNVERIFIED.

**"Ready? You need" and the numbered lessons.** The readiness strip (done / review / unknown, linked to the earlier section) applies mastery logic at the moment of need. A 2024 meta-analysis of pre-questions found benefits only for the content asked about (g = 0.54), not for other content (g = 0.04). Benefits were larger when learners had to produce an answer (g = 0.65) than when they only read the question (g = 0.22) ([prequestion meta-analysis](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/guessing-as-a-learning-intervention-a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-prequestion-effect/903820706179973241-12382); author list to verify). In the mock-up, Chapter 0's "Ready? Three quick ones" shows each answer right under its question ([design mock-up](../design/chem-chapters.html)). **Change** it to answer first, check, then reveal. Numbered, short lessons match the segmenting principle, with a median effect around d = 0.67–0.70 in lab studies ([Mayer principles table](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349)). **Keep.** No phone-specific studies were found.

**Lab link and "at a glance".** Free play alone does not help novices. In a meta-analysis of 164 studies, unassisted discovery did not benefit learners, while guided discovery beat other teaching methods by d = 0.30 ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf)). PhET's design research puts guidance into the simulation itself, through its controls, limits and feedback ([Podolefsky et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.6544)). A large-class chemistry study found such sims usable with no written instructions ([Moore et al. 2013](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2013/rp/c3rp20157k)). **Keep** "Try this", and offer the challenge before free play. "The chapter at a glance" has no direct support: no evidence on *provided* summaries was found, and *learner-made* summaries rate low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). **Consider** keeping it as reference (it doubles as the Tools formula sheet) and adding a "cover and recall" mode that hides each formula until the learner tries it.

**Section page: tabs, fading and three views.** Moving from fully worked to partly worked to solo is the sequence the theory favours. Removing steps from the end first ("backward fading") helped in probability lessons (secondary: [ASU review](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)). In a chemistry tutor, though, worked examples mainly **saved about 21% of the time** for similar learning, compared with tutored practice ([McLaren et al., LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/wiki/index.php?oldid=8825)). Examples are a supplement to practice with feedback, not a replacement. The Simple / Medium / Hard tabs group problems by difficulty. That is fine, but it is not interleaving, which means mixing problem *types* (below). **Keep the tabs.** **Change** two things: fading should respond to how the learner is doing, not to a fixed tab, and each tab needs answer entry with checking. For the three views, Johnstone's three levels (what you see, particles, symbols) describe why chemistry is hard ([Johnstone 1991](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2729.1991.tb00230.x)). Critics call the scheme oversimplified ([Talanquer 2011](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/macro-submicro-and-symbolic-the-many-faces-of-the-chemistry-tripl/)). Multiple representations help mainly when learners are helped to translate between them, which they find hard ([Ainsworth 2006](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577631/deft-a-conceptual-framework-for-considering-learning-with-multiple-representations)). Placing related pieces next to each other also helps (median d ≈ 0.79, [Mayer principles table](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349)). No study compares showing the three side by side with showing them one at a time. **Keep**, and **change** the three boxes from separate panels to linked ones: tap the particles and the matching symbol highlights. This also settles a tension with the project's own principle of showing one level at a time and labelling it ([research summary](../docs/research/README.md)). The views can appear together only if each is labelled and linked.

## Four proven study habits the layout does not yet build

The biggest gap is not the order or any one page element. It is that **the layout has no built-in practice loop**. A major review of study techniques rated **practice testing** (recalling answers) and **distributed practice** (spacing review over time) as the only two "high utility" techniques. It rated rereading, highlighting and summarizing low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). The current design has no recall step after a lesson, and nothing brings back Chapter 4's mole skills during Chapter 7 or Chapter 19. **Interleaving** helps too, at a smaller size. A meta-analysis of 59 studies found an overall effect of g ≈ 0.42, largest for visual categories and small for maths-type tasks ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). No chemistry-specific interleaving study was found. The rule of thumb of practising one type first and mixing later is a reasonable inference, not a sourced finding. **Feedback** is the fourth. Feedback was one of the elements that made guided discovery work ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf)), and worked examples do not beat practice with feedback ([McLaren et al.](https://learnlab.org/wiki/index.php?oldid=8825)). The main feedback meta-analyses (Hattie and Timperley; Wisniewski et al.) were not searched, so the size of the benefit is not given here.

In concrete terms, a section should end with two or three questions the learner must answer, with a unit-aware checker that explains wrong answers. The planned answer entry already flagged in the project's research covers this ([research summary](../docs/research/README.md)). A short "Mixed review" set should mix problem types from earlier chapters, appearing at chapter starts and on Home, which also covers spacing. The "Ready?" strip can do double duty as a recall check if it asks before it reveals. Three smaller additions have less evidence. Self-explanation prompts ("why did we divide here?") were among the helpful elements in the guided-discovery meta-analysis ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf)). Their "moderate utility" rating is from memory and UNVERIFIED. The relevance-writing prompt is covered above. And "predict, then reveal", already planned for graphs in the [design guide](../docs/design-guide.md), could extend to the Lab ("what will happen if you double the temperature?").

## Conclusion

The research reframes what BYE Chem's structure is for. Topic order has weak and inconsistent effects on results. So the bold order choices (maths first, a stand-alone mole chapter, bonds before quantum) are defensible bets that serve the intro course and the "from zero" learner. They are neither proven gains nor real risks, provided the Tro-order Home view keeps class-takers oriented and Chapters 9 and 12 are bridged on purpose. Two small fixes come from the chapter map itself, not from the research: the Intro tag on 0.5 Logarithms, and letting 1A learners reach the equilibrium first look without passing through 1B kinetics.

The bigger finding is where to spend effort next. The evidence-rich habits (recalling answers, spacing, mixing, feedback) all belong to the practice layer, which is still on hold. That layer is BYE Chem's best chance to beat textbooks, not just reorder them. Answer-first "Ready?" checks, checked answers in Simple, Medium and Hard, and a running mixed review would apply the best-supported findings in this report. Debating chapter order further would not. Spot-checks still owed: the CLUE r = 0.6 figure, the ten ACCM concept names, where pH first appears in the general chemistry books, and the mole's place inside the intro books' composition chapters.
