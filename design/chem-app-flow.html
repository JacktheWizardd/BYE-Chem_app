Big 2 in front: how many molecules. You may change it when balancing.
Small 2 below: atoms inside one molecule. Never change it.
Key idea

A mole is a chemist’s dozen. A dozen eggs is 12 eggs; a mole of molecules is 6.022 × 10²³ molecules. The recipe works in moles exactly as it works in molecules.

Why it looks like this

What the research says beginners need

Three levels at once

Chemistry is described at three levels: what you can see, the particles, and the symbols. Experts move between them without noticing; beginners lose their place. So the Learn page keeps one reaction and switches the level, and problems show the particle recipe next to the symbols.

Too much to hold

Working memory is small, and a wordy question uses it up before the chemistry starts. Teachers are advised to strip wordy questions at first and use partly completed, structured calculation layouts. Hence the Have / Want chips, the route bar, and tiles that carry the last result for you.

The usual mix-ups

Coefficients vs subscripts, and using the recipe on grams instead of moles, are the classic stoichiometry slips. The design answers each with form: locked subscripts and coefficient boxes; grams and moles in two colours, with the recipe step only ever between two green (mole) stations.

Units do the thinking

The factor-label method works because units cancel. Drawing each quantity as a tile and striking the cancelled unit makes the check visible: if the unit you have doesn’t cancel, the tile is upside down.

Sources: A. H. Johnstone, “Why is science difficult to learn? Things are seldom what they seem”, Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 7 (1991), on the three levels and working-memory load; M. Parks, How to help students connect maths and chemistry, RSC Education, on stripping wordy questions and faded, structured calculation layouts. The other ideas are common chemistry teaching practice.
