Composition of Substances and Solutions
物質と溶液の組成
A teaspoon of sugar, about 4 g, holds about 7 × 1021 molecules. Nobody counted them. How do we know?
4 sections · Learn and 3 levels each
The story

Atoms are far too small to count, so chemists count by weighing, the way a bank weighs a bag of coins instead of counting them (3.1). The counting unit is the mole: 6.022 × 1023 of anything.

The mole map · 2 of 3

The same counting tells you a compound’s formula from the masses in it (3.2). Then the counting moves into liquids: how many moles in each litre (3.3), and the %, ppm and ppb you see on labels (3.4).

Hospital saline drip
“0.9% saline” is 9 g of salt per litre, which is 0.154 M.
9.0 g ÷ 58.44 g/mol = 0.154 mol
Blood sugar
A normal reading of 90 mg/dL is about 5 millimoles of glucose per litre.
0.90 g/L ÷ 180.16 g/mol = 0.0050 M

Chapter 3 · §3.1
Formula Mass and the Mole Concept
式量とモル
LearnSimpleMediumHard

You can’t count atoms one by one, so you count them by weighing. One mole of anything is 6.022 × 1023 of it, the way one dozen is 12.

The tool
moles = grams ÷ molar mass
Key ideas4
1
Formula mass
Add the masses of every atom in the formula. H2O: 2 × 1.008 + 16.00 = 18.02.
2
The mole is a count
6.022 × 1023 particles, chosen so the numbers line up with grams.
3
Molar mass
The mass of one mole: same number as the formula mass, in grams. Water: 18.02 g/mol.
4
Count by weighing
Divide grams by molar mass and you have moles, then particles.
