# Lesson/chapter page layout for a beginner chemistry app vs. learning-science evidence

Method note: about 17 web searches; full texts were mostly NOT fetched, so many figures come from search summaries or secondary pages and are flagged "secondary". Items marked "(memory)" are from my background knowledge, not verified in this session, and need checking before they are cited. Nothing here is fabricated; unknowns are in Gaps.

## 1. Prerequisite checks, diagnostic placement ("Find your gaps"), mastery learning, pretesting

### Takeaway
Mastery learning (check, remediate, advance) has moderate-to-strong evidence (about 0.5 SD), but mostly for teacher-paced courses, with large variance. ALEKS-style placement/prep in general chemistry looks about as good as a semester prep course, but the strongest claims come from the vendor or vendor-affiliated studies. Pretesting/prequestions help for the material they target (g about 0.54) but not generally (g about 0.04), so a readiness check is best justified as a routing and remediation tool, not as a learning boost on its own.

### Cited Findings
- Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns (1990), Review of Educational Research 60(2), 265-299, synthesized 108 controlled evaluations and found positive effects on exam performance at college, high school and upper elementary levels. A secondary summary gives a mean of about 0.52 SD, which is unverified against the original. — [search summary, secondary](https://typeset.io/pdf/effectiveness-of-mastery-learning-programs-a-meta-analysis-45bgwh1byb.pdf)
- Earlier Kulik analyses: 0.52 (pre-college) and 0.54 (college), as cited by Slavin 1987, who argued that mastery-learning effects are inflated when the tests are experimenter-made. Guskey and Gates (1985) reported larger values (0.94 elementary, 0.72 high school, 0.65 college), which Slavin considered inflated. — [Slavin 1987 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf)
- Guskey and Pigott (1988), J. Educ. Research, a group-based mastery learning meta-analysis, found large heterogeneity across studies. No reliable effect size was retrieved. — [search result, secondary](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/download/article-file/264197)
- Indiana University Bloomington, published in Internet and Higher Education: students who qualified for Principles of Chemistry through ALEKS Prep were as likely to succeed as students who took a semester-long prep course. Group sizes were about 2,561 (on-ground prep course) and 607 (ALEKS Prep), per the vendor summary. — [McGraw-Hill press release (vendor)](https://mheducation.mediaroom.com/2019-12-17-New-Research-Shows-McGraw-Hill-ALEKS-Prepares-Students-for-General-Chemistry-Course-Just-as-Effectively-as-a-Full-Semester-Prep-Intro-Course); [IU spotlight (vendor)](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/highered/documents/aleks/case-studies/chemistry-research-spotlight-aleks-prep-iu-bloomington.pdf)
- Independent: a voluntary ALEKS-based summer module at one institution (Chem. Educ. Res. Pract. 2021) was associated with a marked rise in ACS final-exam percentile for students completing more than 50% of it. This is correlational, since voluntary completion is confounded with motivation. — [CERP 2021](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2021/rp/d0rp00283f)
- Toledo and Oregon case studies (vendor): ALEKS predicted general-chemistry exam performance and replaced a more complex placement method. Treat them as low-rigor. — [Toledo](https://www.mheducation.com/unitas/highered/discipline/chemistry/aleks-case-study-chem-toledo-course-and-placement.pdf); [Oregon](https://www.mheducation.com/unitas/highered/platforms/aleks/aleks-chem-case-study-university-of-oregon.pdf)
- Prequestion meta-analysis (Psychon. Bull. Rev. 2024; authors St. Hilaire et al., per a 2026 citing paper, so verify the author list): g = 0.54 (k = 97) for the specific effect on the prequestioned content, but g = 0.04 (k = 91) for general effects on non-targeted content. Effects were larger when learners had to generate an answer (g = 0.65) than when they only read the question (g = 0.22). — [Bohrium listing](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/guessing-as-a-learning-intervention-a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-prequestion-effect/903820706179973241-12382); [2026 citing paper](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/RBPT_2026.pdf)
- Chan, Meissner and Davis (2018) test-potentiated new learning: g = 0.44 (secondary mention). — [Pan lab page listing](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/YHADP_2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "Ready? You need" strip (2-3 skills marked done/review/unknown) matches the mastery-learning logic (diagnose, remediate, proceed). The evidence supports the principle, though not the specific UI. Mastery studies are mostly classroom or teacher-led, so transfer to a self-paced app is an extrapolation.
- A prequestion-style check helps only for content it asks about. If the prerequisite items mirror what the lesson then teaches, they carry the benefit, so make them generation items (type an answer), not recognition.
- The 10-question "Find your gaps" diagnostic is thin compared with ALEKS, which uses a knowledge-space model with an adaptive question sequence (the knowledge-space background is from memory; not retrieved). Ten questions over a whole chapter list gives coarse routing. Supported as a design direction, but its accuracy is unevidenced for this app.
- Letting learners skip chapters is a risk if the diagnostic false-negatives. The "unknown" state in the readiness strip is a sensible hedge.

### Gaps
- No independent, peer-reviewed RCT of ALEKS placement accuracy was found. Most positive evidence is vendor-affiliated.
- No effect size for Guskey and Pigott 1988 retrieved. The Kulik 0.52 figure is secondary.
- Not found: evidence on short (under 5 item) readiness checks specifically at the start of a lesson in app contexts; knowledge-space theory primary sources (Doignon and Falmagne) not retrieved.
- Bloom's 2-sigma (1984) was not verified in this session; the Bloom PDF appeared in a search result at https://web.mit.edu/5.95/readings/bloom-two-sigma.pdf but was not read. Later work generally finds far smaller effects than 2 SD.

## 2. Goal/utility framing, advance organizers, learning objectives

### Takeaway
Utility-value (UV) interventions have good RCT evidence, but the effective ingredient is the learner writing about personal relevance, not being told uses. Advance organizers have a small, inconsistent evidence base. Static "What this lets you do" and "Where it shows up" content is plausibly an advance organizer/UV prompt, but it is the weaker, told form.

### Cited Findings
- Hulleman and Harackiewicz (2009), Science 326, 1410-1412: randomized field experiment, 262 ninth-grade students, 7 teachers. Students wrote about how science related to their lives (control: summaries). Interest and grades rose, by about two-thirds of a letter grade for students with low success expectations. — [Stanford SPARQ PDF](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/hulleman_harackiewicz_2009_-_promoting_interest_performance_in_high_school_science_classes.pdf); DOI 10.1126/science.1177067 ([replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1177067/))
- Harackiewicz, Canning, Tibbetts, Priniski and Hyde (2016), intro biology, N about 1,040: UV writing raised grades for first-generation, underrepresented-minority students by 0.51 grade points (d about 0.55), narrowing the gap by about 61% per a secondary summary. Check against the published JPSP paper. — [search-result summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5008900)
- The same line of work reports that gains from UV were concentrated in students with low prior success expectations, i.e. effects are conditional. — [Hulleman and Harackiewicz 2009](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/hulleman_harackiewicz_2009_-_promoting_interest_performance_in_high_school_science_classes.pdf)
- A later UV study was recorded as a successful replication (Hulleman et al. 2017, J. Educ. Psych.). — [replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1177067/)
- Related framing caution: telling students about relevance directly can backfire for low-confidence students, because it can signal pressure. This is from the broader UV literature (memory, Canning et al. 2018); I did not retrieve a source.
- Luiten, Ames and Ackerson (1980), AERJ 17(2), 211-218: advance-organizer meta-analysis of 135 studies. A secondary review says aural organizers had an effect size twice that of written ones. The overall pooled effect was not retrieved. A later update ("Thirty years after Ausubel", Mahar 1992) characterized 1960-1980 research as inconclusive. — [Illinois IDEALS](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)

### Inferences
- The chapter-top "What this lets you do" (task plus picture) is supported as a low-cost organizer, but the evidence for written organizers is weak and heterogeneous. Keep it short.
- UV works best when the learner generates the connection. Adding a one-line prompt ("Where could you use this? Write one line") at the end of a chapter is better supported than listing real-world uses. The "Where it shows up" worked numbers may help as concrete examples, but that is not shown by the UV studies.
- Chemistry-specific UV evidence for a self-paced mobile app was not found.

### Gaps
- No pooled effect size for UV interventions retrieved; no app-based UV trial found.
- Overall advance-organizer effect size not retrieved; no chemistry-specific evidence found.
- Learning-objectives literature (e.g. Hamilton 1985; Kurt and Alfieri-type reviews) not searched.

## 3. Johnstone's triplet and multiple representations

### Takeaway
The triplet is a useful descriptive framework, not an evidence-tested layout rule. Multiple representations help under conditions (Ainsworth DeFT), mainly when they complement or constrain each other and when translation between them is supported. Talanquer argues the triplet is oversimplified. Nothing found directly tests "all three side by side vs one at a time".

### Cited Findings
- Johnstone (1991), J. Computer Assisted Learning 7(2), 75-83: argues science is hard because of multilevel thought across macro, submicro and symbolic levels, and that teachers move between levels faster than pupils can follow. — [paper listing](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/why-is-science-difficult-to-learn-things-are-seldom-what-they-seem/811928592706437120-11222); [Glasgow page](https://www.psy.gla.ac.uk/~steve/localed/johnstone.html)
- Talanquer (2011), Int. J. Sci. Educ. 33(2), 179-195: critically assesses assumptions behind various readings of the triplet and proposes a more general structure of chemistry knowledge that places the triplet among types, scales and dimensions. — [Arizona portal](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/macro-submicro-and-symbolic-the-many-faces-of-the-chemistry-tripl/)
- Taber (2013), Chem. Educ. Res. Pract., "Revisiting the chemistry triplet", is a further critique. Retrieved only as a result listing. — [Taber PDF](https://science-education-research.com/downloads/publications/2013/Taber-2013-CERP-chemistry-triplet-AMV.pdf)
- Ainsworth (2006), Learning and Instruction 16, 183-198 (DeFT): multiple representations serve three functions (complement, constrain, construct), with design parameters (number, order, distribution, support for translation) and the cognitive task of translating between representations, which learners often find hard. — [Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577631/deft-a-conceptual-framework-for-considering-learning-with-multiple-representations)
- Mayer-type principles on split attention and spatial contiguity: median d about 0.79 for spatial contiguity per one overview. — [overview](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349)

### Inferences
- Showing See it / Particles / Symbols for one quantity fits DeFT's "complement" function. It risks split attention on a phone if they are in separate blocks, so integrate or link them (for example tap a particle to highlight the matching symbol). This is an inference from DeFT plus spatial contiguity, not a direct test.
- DeFT's task emphasis suggests the app should teach the mapping between the three (labels, color-matched), not just display them.
- Talanquer's critique implies treating "See it / Particles / Symbols" as a heuristic. It omits process/energy dimensions.

### Gaps
- No empirical comparison found of side-by-side vs sequential triplet presentation in chemistry. Not searched in depth: Kozma and Russell (1997, JRST), Gilbert and Treagust 2009. Wu and Shah 2004 and Stieff-type studies of visualization in chemistry not retrieved.

## 4. Worked examples, fading, difficulty tiers, interleaving, retrieval, spacing, feedback

### Takeaway
Worked examples and fading are well supported in theory, but in a real chemistry tutor (stoichiometry) worked examples saved time rather than improving learning gains. Practice testing and distributed practice are the best-supported study techniques (Dunlosky 2013). Blocked Simple/Medium/Hard tiers run against the interleaving evidence (g about 0.42 overall), though for novices a blocked-first sequence is defensible.

### Cited Findings
- McLaren, Lim and Koedinger (2008), stoichiometry cognitive tutor, three studies: worked examples used about 21% less time with similar learning. McLaren and Isotani (2011) and McLaren et al. (2014, high school; worked, tutored, untutored, erroneous examples) found no significant gain differences, with worked-example groups faster. All secondary via LearnLab pages. — [LearnLab wiki](https://learnlab.org/wiki/index.php?oldid=8825); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10472800)
- Backward fading (Renkl, Atkinson and colleagues, 2002/2004, probability/statistics) had positive effects. Only secondary mentions retrieved. — [ASU review](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)
- Barbieri and Miller-Cotto (2023), Educ. Psych. Rev.: meta-analysis of worked examples in mathematics; effect size not retrieved. — [search result](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1293516/pdf)
- Brunmair and Richter (2019), Psychol. Bull. 145(11), 1029-1052: 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples; overall interleaving effect Hedges g about 0.42; benefits depend on material (largest for paintings/visual categories, g = 0.67); a small significant effect for mathematical tasks. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10518320)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013), Psychol. Sci. Public Interest 14(1), 4-58, DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266: high utility = practice testing and distributed practice; low utility = summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. (Moderate-utility techniques: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaving; from memory.) — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [DOI listing](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/improving-students-learning-with-effective-learning-techniques)
- Alfieri et al. (2011), J. Educ. Psych.: 164 studies; unassisted discovery does not benefit learners; enhanced discovery d = 0.30 (CI .23-.36) vs other instruction; feedback, worked examples, scaffolding and elicited explanations were the helpful elements. — [UU copy](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf)

### Inferences
- A fully worked, partly worked, solo progression is the fading design the theory favors. Expect more efficiency than raw learning gain in some tutors (McLaren). Fading should be performance-driven rather than tied to a fixed "Simple/Medium/Hard" tab.
- Difficulty tabs are blocked by level. The interleaving evidence concerns mixing problem types, not difficulty levels. A "Mixed review" set that interleaves problem types across sections (with spacing from earlier chapters) is the missing, better-supported element. For beginners, blocked first practice then interleaved is the common recommendation (inference; no source retrieved).
- The app has no stated retrieval practice, spaced review or feedback design. These are the highest-utility techniques per Dunlosky and are the main layout gap.

### Gaps
- No chemistry-specific interleaving study retrieved; Rohrer's classroom math studies not retrieved. Renkl and Atkinson primary papers and their effect sizes not retrieved. Feedback meta-analyses (Hattie and Timperley 2007; Wisniewski et al. 2020) not searched.

## 5. Simulations with guidance (PhET)

### Takeaway
PhET research supports simulations whose design embeds guidance (implicit scaffolding), with light written guidance. This supports short goal-directed "Try this" challenges, while the meta-analytic evidence (Alfieri) cautions against pure free play for novices.

### Cited Findings
- Podolefsky, Moore and Perkins (arXiv 1306.6544): implicit scaffolding framework built from about 10 years of PhET design, 125 sims and over 600 student interviews; guidance lives in affordances, constraints and feedback rather than in written instructions. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.6544)
- Chamberlain, Lancaster, Parson and Perkins (2014), CERP: Acid-Base Solutions sim with heavy, moderate and light written guidance; hypothesis that implicit scaffolding lets students with minimal guidance engage. Results were not retrieved. — [CERP 2014](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2014/rp/c4rp00009a)
- Moore, Herzog and Perkins (2013), CERP: in a large chemistry class a sim with implicit scaffolding and no explicit instructions could be used through exploration. — [CERP 2013](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2013/rp/c3rp20157k)
- Alfieri et al. (2011): unassisted discovery is not beneficial; guided is (d = 0.30). See section 4.

### Inferences
- Pairing a sandbox with one short "Try this" challenge is in line with guided exploration. Place the challenge before free play, and check how much of the benefit relies on the sim's own scaffolding.
- Outcomes of light vs heavy guidance in the Chamberlain study were not retrieved, so the "right amount" is unknown.

### Gaps
- No data retrieved on challenge-first vs free-play-first ordering; no mobile-specific sim studies; Wieman, Adams and Perkins 2008 (Science) not retrieved.

## 6. Multimedia design for small screens; summaries/"at a glance"

### Takeaway
Mayer's reduction-of-extraneous-processing principles (coherence, signaling, segmenting, contiguity) have consistent medium-to-large effects in lab studies. Mobile-specific evidence was not found. Summarization as a study technique is low-utility, but that concerns learner-generated summaries.

### Cited Findings
- Median effects from Mayer (2021), as tabulated: coherence d = 0.86 (18 of 19 tests positive), signaling 0.69 (15 of 16), segmenting 0.67. An earlier overview gives coherence 0.70, signaling 0.46, segmenting 0.70, redundancy 0.87, spatial contiguity 0.79. Sources differ, so state which version is cited. — [edtechbooks table](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349); [overview](https://ksvi.mff.cuni.cz/amulab/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/MultimLn3_BromHannemann_181117.pdf)
- A meta-analysis of 92 articles, 181 studies and 591 effects (1990-2022) found the largest effects for removing seductive details, modality, personalization, multimedia and self-explanation; cueing had a medium effect. — [search result](https://www.synapsesocial.com/es/papers/69dbbbdacebd566818835a69)
- Dunlosky et al. 2013 rated summarization and highlighting low-utility. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)

### Inferences
- Section pages that combine intro, tool, key ideas, triplet, lab and links on one page risk coherence violations. Segmenting (one idea per screen/scroll block, learner-paced) fits the numbered key ideas.
- "Chapter at a glance" formula summary: evidence on provided summaries or concept maps was not retrieved, so its value is unsupported. It is more defensible as a reference (as with the formula sheet) than as a learning step. Converting it into a recall practice item would be better supported (Dunlosky).

### Gaps
- No mobile-learning or small-screen study retrieved (split attention on phones). No source on concept maps or provided summaries was retrieved (Nesbit and Adesope 2006 is a candidate, memory only).

## 7. What successful chemistry platforms do (brief)

### Takeaway
Not researched in primary sources. Only the ALEKS findings from section 1 are sourced.

### Cited Findings
- ALEKS: adaptive assessment determines what a student is ready to learn, and it is used for placement and prep. Evidence base is largely vendor-affiliated. — [McGraw-Hill](https://mheducation.mediaroom.com/2019-12-17-New-Research-Shows-McGraw-Hill-ALEKS-Prepares-Students-for-General-Chemistry-Course-Just-as-Effectively-as-a-Full-Semester-Prep-Intro-Course)
- West Virginia University (J. Chem. Educ.): a four-year study of 8,500+ students compared adaptive vs traditional online homework (per the search summary, not verified). — [search summary](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2021/rp/d0rp00283f)

### Inferences
- ALEKS's structural features (diagnostic then a personalized path, ongoing re-assessment) resemble the app's "Find your gaps", which lacks the ongoing re-assessment.

### Gaps
- Khan Academy, OpenStax Chemistry: Atoms First, Sapling and Mastering structures not researched in this pass.
