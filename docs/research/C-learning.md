# C. How beginners learn chemistry: research notes for BYE Chem

Compiled 2026-11-06. Method: web searches and Crossref record lookups; most papers were NOT read in full, so findings below rest on abstracts and citing summaries unless stated. Anything I could not confirm is marked **UNVERIFIED**. Evidence strength: **Strong** (replicated, multiple studies), **Moderate**, **Weak/mixed**.

---

## 1. Johnstone's three levels and the working-memory model

**Citations (checked)**
- Johnstone, A. H. (1991). Why is science difficult to learn? Things are seldom what they seem. *J. Computer Assisted Learning* 7, 75-83. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2729.1991.tb00230.x (Crossref record confirms title, volume, pages.)
- Johnstone, A. H. (1993). *J. Chem. Educ.* 70(9), 701. https://doi.org/10.1021/ed070p701 . DOI, volume and first page confirmed by a search; the title I was given ("The development of chemistry teaching: a changing response to changing demand") was **not independently confirmed**, and page range is reported inconsistently (701-705 vs a typo).
- Johnstone, A. H. (2000). Teaching of chemistry - logical or psychological? *Chem. Educ. Res. Pract.* 1, 9-15. https://doi.org/10.1039/A9RP90001B (confirmed; abstract says learners struggle to hold the three levels at once).
- Johnstone, A. H. "You can't get there from here", *J. Chem. Educ.* 87(1), 22-29. https://doi.org/10.1021/ed800026d . Crossref lists year 2009 (online) with volume 87 (2010 issue): cite as 2010 (print), note 2009 online date.
- Johnstone, A. H. & El-Banna, H. (1986). Capacities, demands and processes - a predictive model for science education. *Education in Chemistry* 23, 80-84. Citation confirmed via secondary sources; no DOI found; full text not seen. Reported finding: success on chemistry problems tracked working-memory capacity relative to the demand of the task.
- Johnstone 1982 "Macro and micro chemistry", *School Science Review* (often cited as the origin of the triangle): **UNVERIFIED**, not located.

**Findings (own words)**
- Chemistry is understood at three levels: what you can see and measure (macroscopic), the particles and their behaviour (submicroscopic), and the symbols, formulas, equations and maths (symbolic). Experts move between them freely; beginners get overloaded when a lesson uses all three at once without saying which one is in play.
- Working memory holds only a handful of items at once. When a step makes the learner juggle several new things, success collapses; Johnstone's suggestion is to introduce one level at a time, keep the load per step small, and make links between levels explicit.
- Johnstone (2000) argues a "psychological" order (what a learner can hold) should shape sequencing, rather than only the "logical" order of the subject.

**Strength:** The three-level framing is very widely used and well supported as a descriptive model (**Strong** as a framework). The specific working-memory-overload causal claim is **Moderate**: supported by Johnstone's own correlational studies and consistent with cognitive load theory, but much of it is from his Glasgow group.

## 2. Particulate-level understanding and particle diagrams

**Citations**
- Nakhleh, M. B. (1992). Why some students don't learn chemistry: chemical misconceptions. *J. Chem. Educ.* 69(3), 191. https://doi.org/10.1021/ed069p191 (Crossref confirmed.)
- Nurrenbern, S. C. & Pickering, M. (1987). Concept learning versus problem solving: is there a difference? *J. Chem. Educ.* 64(6), 508-510. https://doi.org/10.1021/ed064p508 (confirmed via citing sources; original text not read). Follow-ups: Pickering (1990) *J. Chem. Educ.* 67, 254-255 and Sawrey (1990) *J. Chem. Educ.* 67, 253-254 (titles/pages from a search; DOIs not checked).
- Yarroch, W. L. (1985). Student understanding of chemical equation balancing. *J. Res. Sci. Teach.* 22(5), 449-459. https://doi.org/10.1002/tea.3660220507 (abstract seen via Michigan Tech record).
- Sanger, M. J. (2000). Using particulate drawings to determine and improve students' conceptions of pure substances and mixtures. *J. Chem. Educ.* 77(6), 762-766. https://doi.org/10.1021/ed077p762 (abstract level).
- Gabel, D. (1999). Improving teaching and learning through chemistry education research: a look to the future. *J. Chem. Educ.* 76(4), 548. https://doi.org/10.1021/ed076p548 (catalogue abstract only; she argues the particulate level is where instruction is most needed).

**Findings**
- Nurrenbern & Pickering: students who could solve standard quantitative problems often failed matching conceptual questions that asked for particle pictures of the same chemistry. Being able to run an algorithm does not show understanding. Later work in the same line replicated the gap in various topics.
- Yarroch: in a small sample (12 students), all could balance equations, but 7 could not draw a particle picture matching the balanced equation, and those students were willing to change subscripts. Balancing can be done as pattern-matching.
- Sanger: students taught with particle drawings of mixtures and pure substances did better on identifying particle pictures than traditionally taught students (abstract-level; single course context).
- Nakhleh's review frames many difficulties as arising from missing or incorrect particle models.

**Strength:** The algorithmic-versus-conceptual gap is **Strong** (many replications). That particle drawings help is **Moderate** (positive studies but small, topic-specific; mostly drawing/visualisation, not full apps).

## 3. Common misconceptions

**Citations**
- Kind, V. (2004). *Beyond Appearances: Students' misconceptions about basic chemical ideas* (2nd ed.). Royal Society of Chemistry. Covers eleven areas incl. states of matter, particle theory, bonding, stoichiometry, equilibrium. https://edu.rsc.org/resources/beyond-appearances/2202.article (page confirmed; I read only the listing, not the whole report).
- Taber, K. S. (2002). *Chemical Misconceptions: Prevention, Diagnosis and Cure, Vol. 1 Theoretical Background*. RSC. ISBN 978-0-85404-386-6. https://books.rsc.org/books/monograph/2049 (listing confirmed; one catalogue says 2005, RSC says 2002).
- Furio, C., Azcona, R., Guisasola, J. & Ratcliffe, M. (2000). Difficulties in teaching the concepts of 'amount of substance' and 'mole'. *Int. J. Sci. Educ.* 22(12), 1285-1304. https://doi.org/10.1080/095006900750036262 (DOI from catalogue pages that agree; publisher page not opened). Also review: Furio et al. (2002) *CERP* https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2002/rp/b2rp90023h (title seen in results; authors not confirmed).
- Andersson, B. (1984 and 1990): students and conservation of mass in reactions; summarised at https://edu.rsc.org/resources/students-ideas-about-specific-chemical-events-closed-system-reactions-beyond-appearances/4017790.article and https://spark.iop.org/andersson-1990 . Original papers not located with DOIs.
- Mitchell & Gunstone (1984) on conservation of mass: **UNVERIFIED**.
- Ralph, V. R. & Lewis, S. E. (2018). Chemistry topics posing incommensurate difficulty to students with low math aptitude scores. *CERP* 19(3), 867-884. https://doi.org/10.1039/C8RP00115D (author spelling varies across records; check the article page).

**Findings**
- Coefficients vs subscripts: some students change subscripts to balance (Yarroch, above). Treat as a known, replicated trap.
- Conservation of mass: roughly a third of 15-year-olds in the Andersson sealed-flask work predicted mass would not stay the same; reasons included smoke or gas "weighing nothing" and matter "used up" (RSC summary of Andersson 1984). Related belief: substance disappears in reactions (Andersson 1990 via IOP SPARK).
- The mole: Furio et al. trace the difficulty to how "amount of substance" and the mole were historically and in teaching blurred together with mass and number of particles. Mole concept is hard for the whole mixed-ability class.
- At-risk (low SAT-math) students in a large first-semester chemistry sample (N = 5636): struggles were not confined to algebra-heavy topics; those who did well on mole concept and stoichiometry went on to perform similarly to peers, so the authors suggest emphasising these topics (Ralph & Lewis 2018, abstract-level).
- Bonding and gases: I did not retrieve primary evidence in this pass. Özmen (2004) is a bonding misconceptions literature review, *J. Sci. Educ. Technol.* 13, 147-159, https://doi.org/10.1023/b:jost.0000031255.92943.6d (Crossref confirmed, not read). Gas misconceptions: a brief IOP SPARK page lists "gases do not have mass" ( https://spark.iop.org/some-students-think-gases-do-not-have-mass ); weak, secondary.

**Strength:** Existence of misconceptions: **Strong**. Prevalence figures: **Moderate**, dated, and age/country dependent (school students in the main). Effective remedies: **Weak/mixed**; Kind (2004) catalogues them, but cautionary commentary exists on how little this research has changed practice (see https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2014/rp/c4rp90009j, title only seen).

## 4. Worked examples, fading, self-explanation

**Citations**
- Sweller, J. & Cooper, G. A. (1985). The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra. *Cognition and Instruction* 2(1), 59-89. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3
- Sweller, J., van Merrienboer, J. J. G. & Paas, F. G. W. C. (1998). Cognitive architecture and instructional design. *Educ. Psychol. Rev.* 10, 251-296. https://doi.org/10.1023/a:1022193728205 ; updated: Sweller et al. (2019) *Educ. Psychol. Rev.* 31, 261-292, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5 (both Crossref confirmed).
- Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P. & Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. *Educational Psychologist* 38(1), 23-31. Citation confirmed; **DOI not retrieved (UNVERIFIED)**.
- Renkl, A., Atkinson, R. K., Maier, U. H. & Staley, R. (2002). From example study to problem solving: smooth transitions help learning. *J. Experimental Education* 70(4), 293-315. Citation confirmed through a reference list; **DOI not retrieved (UNVERIFIED)**. Idea: remove solution steps gradually (fading); studied in a physics lesson with ninth graders.
- McLaren, B. M., Lim, S. & Koedinger, K. R. (2008). When and how often should worked examples be given to students? New results and a summary of the current state of research. *Proc. 30th Annual Conf. Cognitive Science Society*, 2176-2181. Citation confirmed through reference lists; **full text not seen, no DOI**.
- McLaren et al. stoichiometry tutor work, overview: https://learnlab.org/wiki/index.php?oldid=7310 (Studying the learning effect of personalization and worked examples in solving stoichiometry problems). A 2014 follow-up compared worked examples, tutored and untutored problem solving, and erroneous examples in high-school stoichiometry; **outcomes not retrieved (UNVERIFIED)**.
- Crippen, K. J. & Earl, B. L. (2007). The impact of web-based worked examples and self-explanation on performance, problem solving, and self-efficacy. *Computers & Education* 49(3), 809-821. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2005.11.018 (Crossref confirmed; the study is in a chemistry setting per my recollection but I did not confirm that from the abstract: **UNVERIFIED** as to subject).

**Findings**
- Worked-example effect: for novices, studying solved examples beats solving equivalent problems unaided, at least on similar problems, because it frees working memory for building a schema (Sweller & Cooper 1985; Sweller et al. 1998).
- Expertise reversal: the same support can become unhelpful or harmful as learners get skilled, so scaffolding should fade (Kalyuga et al. 2003).
- Fading: moving from full examples to examples with the last step missing, then more missing, is a recommended transition (Renkl et al. 2002); evidence from outside chemistry.
- Stoichiometry tutor (McLaren, Lim, Koedinger 2008): alternating worked examples with tutored problems did not raise learning beyond tutored problems alone, but the example group reached the same learning more efficiently (less time). Important nuance: gains over *unaided* problem solving are bigger than over *tutored* problem solving.
- Self-explanation prompts: Crippen & Earl (2007) combine worked examples and self-explanation; I did not extract effect sizes.

**Strength:** Worked-example and expertise-reversal effects: **Strong** in cognitive-load literature, mostly in maths, physics, algebra; chemistry-specific evidence is **Moderate/limited**. Fading: **Moderate**. Showing steps one at a time is consistent with these effects but I found no study testing step-by-step reveal as a UI pattern: **Weak (extrapolation)**.

## 5. Problem solving: algorithmic vs conceptual; dimensional analysis vs proportional reasoning

**Citations**
- Nurrenbern & Pickering 1987 (section 2). Bodner on chemistry problem solving: **UNVERIFIED**, not located in this pass.
- Johnstone & El-Banna 1986 (section 1): problem success vs working-memory demand.
- A 1990s study comparing four methods for the mole concept and gas laws by proportional-reasoning ability: result as summarised in a search result: factor-label best for mole concept, proportionality best for gas laws, with success depending on proportional-reasoning ability. The record behind the result: https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/tea.3660200207 ; **authors, year and journal were not confirmed from the page: UNVERIFIED** (the DOI string suggests *J. Res. Sci. Teach.* 20(2), i.e. 1983, which also does not match "1990s"; treat as unclear).
- Wagner, E. P. (2001). A study comparing the efficacy of a mole ratio flow chart to dimensional analysis for teaching reaction stoichiometry. *School Science and Mathematics* (title and venue from a search; findings not retrieved, **UNVERIFIED**).
- Critiques of factor-label: Canagaratna (1993) "Is dimensional analysis the best we have to offer?" and DeToma (1994) "Symbolic algebra and stoichiometry", both *J. Chem. Educ.*; seen only as reference-list entries in https://journals.unam.mx/index.php/req/article/view/88260 (textbook analysis arguing for an algebraic alternative). Primary DOIs not retrieved (**UNVERIFIED**).
- Pre-service teacher study: https://unis.ahievran.edu.tr/yayin-detay/2_CZCvCpO_0/examining-strategies-used-by-pre-service-science-teachers-in-stoichiometry-problems-in-terms-of-proportional-reasoning (secondary page).

**Findings**
- Algorithms can be run without understanding (section 2), so an app should pair each calculation step with a particle or "why" cue.
- Stoichiometry is a proportional-reasoning task. Dimensional analysis (unit-cancelling chains) works well as a procedure and keeps units visible, but critics say it can hide the proportional structure. Evidence on which is better is sparse, old and mixed.

**Strength:** Algorithmic/conceptual split: **Strong**. Dimensional analysis vs proportional reasoning: **Weak/mixed**; I could not find a recent, well-controlled comparison. Do not claim either is proven superior.

## 6. Multiple representations and visualisation tools

**Citations**
- Kozma, R. B. & Russell, J. (1997). Multimedia and understanding: expert and novice responses to different representations of chemical phenomena. *J. Res. Sci. Teach.* 34(9), 949-968. https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1098-2736(199711)34:9<949::AID-TEA7>3.0.CO;2-U (Crossref confirmed). Follow-up chapter: Russell & Kozma (2005) https://doi.org/10.1007/1-4020-3613-2_15
- Moore, E. B., Chamberlain, J. M., Parson, R. & Perkins, K. K. (2014). PhET interactive simulations: transformative tools for teaching chemistry. *J. Chem. Educ.* 91(8), 1191-1197. https://doi.org/10.1021/ed4005084 (from a search; the author list given there omits Wieman, which is fine; abstract-level).
- Perkins, Lancaster, Loeblein, Parson, Podolefsky (2010), PhET chemistry paper (venue not confirmed; listing at https://science.widener.edu/svb/cccenews/fall2010/paper7.html). A direct Wieman-authored chemistry-PhET outcomes paper: **UNVERIFIED**.
- Gilbert & Treagust (2009), *Multiple Representations in Chemical Education* (chapter 8 on diagrams; seen via https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-1-4020-8872-8_9 ; the book is the same Springer ISBN 978-1-4020-8872-8 that appears in search results).

**Findings**
- Kozma & Russell: novices tend to reason from the surface look of a representation (colours, shapes), while experts reason in terms of underlying chemistry and move between representations. This is why a coloured picture alone can mislead beginners.
- PhET: Moore et al. present sims such as Molecule Polarity and Beer's Law Lab as research-informed tools usable from school to college. They describe design and use; this is not a controlled outcomes study.
- Animation of particles combined with observable phenomena is reported to help (secondary reviews). The original Sanger/Phelps/Fienhold (2000) animation paper I tried to confirm **could not be found: UNVERIFIED**.

**Strength:** Novice surface-feature reasoning: **Moderate-Strong**. Benefits of interactive simulations: **Moderate** (positive studies exist; design quality matters).

## 7. Adult/returning learners, math anxiety, and prerequisite maths

**Citations**
- Williamson, V. M. et al. (2020). Impact of basic arithmetic skills on success in first-semester general chemistry. *CERP* 21, 51-61. https://doi.org/10.1039/C9RP00077A (abstract-level). N = 1073; a 15-minute calculator-free arithmetic test (MUST) correlates with course grades (reported r about 0.29-0.54 across studies) and, with demographics, flags students likely to score below 69.5%.
- Ralph & Lewis 2018 (section 3).
- A follow-up where a one-week maths review raised MUST scores more than a chemistry review: Mamiya et al., cited at https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4N0x8me9 (title seen; details **UNVERIFIED**).
- Chemistry anxiety and self-efficacy: Turkish study of 518 students, *Educational Psychology* 31(1), 55-66, 2011, https://avesis.metu.edu.tr/yayin/87c1e5a8-b542-4565-8444-eaa6872506a8/interplay-of-motivational-and-cognitive-strategies-in-predicting-self-efficacy-and-anxiety (authors not captured; lowering chemistry anxiety linked to higher self-efficacy; correlational).
- Spencer (1996) "Correlates of success in college chemistry": **UNVERIFIED**. Hovey & Krohn (1963): **UNVERIFIED**.

**Findings**
- Basic arithmetic fluency (fractions, negatives, powers of ten, mental maths) and maths aptitude consistently predict general chemistry performance. Being weak at algebra does not explain the whole gap.
- At-risk students who master mole concept and stoichiometry catch up (Ralph & Lewis 2018), so those topics are worth the investment.
- Math anxiety: I found no chemistry-education journal paper establishing its effect size in general chemistry, and none on returning adult learners specifically. A STEM-wide result is that lower maths self-efficacy goes with higher maths anxiety; correlational. Treat claims about adult returners as **not established in this search**.

**Strength:** Maths skill predicts chemistry grades: **Strong**. Math anxiety as a mechanism in chemistry: **Weak**. Adult/returning learner specifics: **No evidence found**.

## 8. Accessibility

**Citations**
- W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (WCAG 2.2, Level A): do not rely on colour alone for information. https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html (fetched).
- WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), AA: 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text; SC 1.4.4 Resize Text, AA: usable at 200%; SC 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), AA: 24 by 24 CSS px (new in 2.2); 2.5.5 AAA uses 44 by 44. Confirmed from several secondary guides only; **I did not fetch the W3C spec text for these (UNVERIFIED against primary)**. Spec home: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/
- Colour-vision deficiency prevalence: about 8% of men and 0.5% of women is a common approximation; sources found were low quality. NEI page returned 404. Treat as a rough figure, **UNVERIFIED against a primary source**.
- CPK colour conventions and colour-blind safety: no source found. Known (general knowledge, not cited): CPK uses red for oxygen and blue for nitrogen, with greens and reds both common, which are problematic for red-green deficiency. **UNVERIFIED**.

**Findings**
- Never let colour carry meaning alone: label atoms with element symbols and use shape, size or pattern as a second cue. Check text contrast at 4.5:1, allow 200% text size, keep touch targets at least 24 px (44 px for comfortable mobile use).

**Strength:** WCAG basics are standards, not research; the standard is **Strong** as a requirement. Evidence that CPK palettes specifically harm learners: **not found**.

## Design principles (8-12), with evidence strength

1. **Show one level at a time and label it** (macro / particle / symbol) when switching; link them explicitly. Basis: Johnstone 1991, 2000 (sec. 1). Strength: Strong as framework, Moderate as causal.
2. **Keep each revealed step small; one new idea per step** to stay within working memory. Basis: Johnstone & El-Banna 1986; Sweller et al. 1998. Strength: Moderate-Strong.
3. **Start with fully worked examples for novices, then fade** to partial steps and finally full problems. Basis: Sweller & Cooper 1985; Renkl et al. 2002; Kalyuga et al. 2003. Strength: Strong in general, Moderate in chemistry.
4. **Do not assume examples beat tutored practice**; if the app gives hints and feedback, examples mainly save time. Basis: McLaren, Lim, Koedinger 2008. Strength: Moderate.
5. **Pair every calculation with a particle picture or a "why" check** so students cannot succeed by algorithm alone. Basis: Nurrenbern & Pickering 1987; Yarroch 1985; Sanger 2000. Strength: Strong for the gap, Moderate for the fix.
6. **Draw balanced equations with atom-count particle pictures; treat subscripts as fixed** and warn when a learner edits one. Basis: Yarroch 1985. Strength: Moderate (small study, widely echoed).
7. **Build explicit conservation-of-mass checks into reaction examples**, including gases and sealed systems. Basis: Andersson via RSC/IOP summaries; Kind 2004. Strength: Moderate (school-age samples).
8. **Teach the mole as a counting unit with a bridge between mass, amount and particles, and give it extra time.** Basis: Furio et al. 2000; Ralph & Lewis 2018. Strength: Moderate.
9. **Show units at every step; offer proportional reasoning ("for every...") alongside unit-cancelling** and do not claim either is superior. Basis: mixed evidence (sec. 5). Strength: Weak.
10. **Include a short, calculator-free arithmetic refresher (fractions, powers of ten, negatives)** and make maths prerequisites visible. Basis: Williamson et al. 2020; Ralph & Lewis 2018. Strength: Strong that maths predicts success, Moderate that a refresher helps.
11. **Make pictures teach, not decorate**: add symbols/labels and a caption of what to notice, because novices read surface features. Basis: Kozma & Russell 1997. Strength: Moderate-Strong.
12. **Never use colour alone**; keep body text at 4.5:1 contrast, support 200% text size, and use at least 24 px (prefer 44 px) targets. Basis: WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.1, 1.4.3, 1.4.4, 2.5.8. Strength: Strong (standard), though CPK-specific learning evidence is absent.
